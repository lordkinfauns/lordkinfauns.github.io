About

This is an unofficial fan website devoted to the history of the Lords of Kinfauns. Few titles have a story to match it. It starts with a pardoned pirate on the Tay, passes through some of the great houses of Scotland, and is held today at Newhall House, beside the ruins of Killone Abbey in County Clare. I am a web designer with an interest in this history, and I built the site out of enthusiasm for such subjects and as a showcase of my work. If you like it and would like a website of your own, get in touch at fan@lordkinfauns.com. It is not written or approved by Lord Kinfauns, and the family’s official website is kinfauns.co.

History

Lordship of Kinfauns

The paper trail starts in 1487, when the lordship was confirmed under King James III, and it was confirmed again in 1608 under King James VI, who was also King of Ireland. Both were confirmations of an older creation. The earliest charter known, by which King David II granted half of the lands of Kinfauns in 1330, survives in a private collection. Legend adds the best part, and credits that first grant to a onetime sea pirate, Sir Thomas Charteris, who was pardoned and knighted. His story is told at length elsewhere on this site. The line of succession later merged into the Earldom of Moray, one of the seven ancient earldoms of Scotland, and stayed there for generations before devolving upon the Commane family under the succession provisions of the original charter.

The lordship comes with a hereditary office of its own, the Admiralty of the Tay, granted by the Crown upon the heirs and successors of the lordship of Kinfauns and a reminder of how the barony once commanded the river in Perthshire. Tradition says ships passing Kinfauns Castle dipped their flags to it.
