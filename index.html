Lord Kinfauns

Timeline

Twenty-six lords across seven centuries. The lordship of Kinfauns begins with a pardoned French pirate, the Red Reaver. It then passes through Charteris knights, a Lindsay earl, the Hays, the Blairs, the Grays and the Earls of Moray, largely one kindred linked by descent and marriage, and it is held today at Newhall in County Clare. The whole run is set out below, lord by lord.

The Pirate
"Red Reaver"
Sir Thomas Charteris

Every good title needs a founding legend, and Kinfauns has one of the best: its first lord was a pirate. According to legend the first lord of Kinfauns was Sir Thomas de Longueville, later named Charteris. He was a native of France, and representative of a family well known in that country; but at the close of the thirteenth century, when at the court of Philip the Fair, having a dispute with one of the noblemen, he slew him in the king’s presence. Being refused pardon for the rash and bloody act, he betook himself to the high seas, and under the name of the Red Reaver, became terrible to all seafaring people as a remorseless pirate.

Sir William Wallace, however, in his voyage to France, having met and encountered this formidable giaour, took him prisoner, and pleased with that kindred spirit of heroism, which would have immortalised the reaver in a better cause, presented him to the French king as a suppliant for mercy. Philip, who was pleased to have an occasion of obliging the Scottish hero, and of exhibiting a signal instance of his royal clemency, cancelled the sentence of outlawry, and conferred on his penitent subject the honour of knighthood.

Thus restored to court favour, and to society, Charteris accompanied Wallace to Scotland, where he ever after remained his steady friend, and a frank participator in all his daring exploits. When Wallace was betrayed and carried into England, and Bruce asserted his right to the Scottish crown, Charteris was the first that followed him into the water at the taking of Perth, in January 1313, and in reward for his bravery, received a royal grant of estate and title Lord of Kinfauns. The charter that survives is dated 1330, the year after Bruce died. In it King David II, Bruce’s son, granted Thomas de Carnoto, the Latin form of Charteris, half of the lands of Kinfauns for his homage and good service, and it is still held in a private collection. Bruce had earlier given the same half to a Milanese, Master Maninus de Maneriis, who handed it back to the Crown. He was very likely Maino de Maineri, the physician who attended Robert the Bruce.

Sir William Charteris

The pirate’s son kept what his father had won. Sir William, 2nd of Kinfauns, succeeded his father in the half of Kinfauns. In 1378 he also had a charter of confirmation from James Douglas, son and heir of Sir William, Earl of Douglas, for the lands of Lumphanan in Aberdeenshire. Those had been granted by John Randolph, Earl of Moray, to his uncle Sir Patrick de Carnoto, who seems to have died without children, and the grant was confirmed again by Robert Stewart, Earl of Fife, on 17 March 1391. Note the Earl of Moray in that chain. It is not the last time the name appears on this page.

Sir Thomas Charteris of Cagnore

With Sir Thomas the paper trail gets firmer. He had a charter for the half lands of Kinfauns as heir to his father on 5 April 1408. On 10 July 1412 Robert de Ross of Craigie gave him a licence to take water through his lands, and in it calls him his “beloved brother”, a small human touch across six centuries.

He resigned his lands of Grubet, Yhethame, Corbet, Holden and Turifmuirside, all in Roxburghshire, to Nicholas de Rutherford on 12 May 1426. He died some time after that and before October 1443.

Sir William Charteris of Cagnore

Sir William was served heir to his grandfather, Thomas Charteris, in the lands of Lumphanan on 1 October 1443, and he married three times. One wife was Christian, daughter of Sir Robert, 1st Lord Erskine, and widow of Patrick, 1st Lord Graham, who had died in 1465; she is named with him in a complaint dated 9 July 1476.

Another was Agnes Cochrane, his wife by 13 July 1470, to whom his charter of that date reserved a widow’s third of the lands. Elizabeth Stewart survived him and is styled his relict on 31 October 1479. He died after 3 February 1479 and before 31 October of that year.

de jure

1st Lord Kinfauns
Sir Thomas Charteris

This is the moment the title enters the record. The crown charter of 13 July 1470, held in a private collection, uses the style dominus Kynfawnis, Lord Kinfauns. By it his father conveyed to him the lands of Haltoun, Craigton, Pitmorche and Craigour, all in the lordship of Lumphanan, and those of Kinfauns and Pitsindy in the lordship of Kinfauns. On 15 June 1487 King James III confirmed it under the Great Seal, and the published register describes the lands as lying in dominio de Kynfawnis, in the lordship of Kinfauns. The family had held half of Kinfauns by charter since 1330.

He had married Euphemia Bruce by 5 November 1471, and the marriage came with a family row. His father took instruments warning the couple to surrender the lands of Craigquarter and Little Cagnor under a letter of reversion, so that he could infeft them in the £20 lands of Cagnor instead. They did not comply, and on 19 November Sir William took the lands back by the old ceremony of “taking of earth and stone from the ground there.” Sir Thomas died before October 1520.

2nd Lord Kinfauns
Sir John Charteris

Sir John first appears as son and heir of Sir Thomas Charteris of Kinfauns on 17 September 1506, witnessing the charter by which William Comyn of Inverallochy gave half of his lands of Audiall to the University of Aberdeen. He was served heir to his father in the lands of Kinfauns on 2 October 1520.

By July 1519 he had married Euphemia, daughter of David Lindsay, 8th Earl of Crawford, and his father gave the couple a charter for the lands of Haltoun and Craigton of Lumphanan. With her he also had a charter of confirmation under the Great Seal for Golkton, Ribno and Tulyhow in Perthshire and Little Cagnore in Stirlingshire, on 17 March 1525. He died after that date and by 18 August 1534.

What followed is the darkest episode in the whole timeline. His son Thomas Charteris was outlawed, so the King never recognised his title. Thomas was infeft in the lands of Cagnoir as heir to his father on 18 August 1534, and they were apprised from him on 15 May 1545 for the murder of James Ross and the ruin of the mill of Craigton, which belonged to Ross and his wife, Marjorie Stewart. In 1544 Thomas, John Charteris and about eighty others were proclaimed rebels in the fight with Lord Ruthven over who should be provost of Perth. He was served heir to his grandfather, Thomas Charteris, in the lands of Lumphanan on 17 April 1546. He married first Janet Stewart, from whom he separated, and secondly Katherine, sister of John Graham, 3rd Earl of Montrose, and widow of Sir James Chisholm, 2nd of Cromlix. Katherine is styled his relict in an action she raised against Janet Stewart, the first wife, on 20 March 1556. She later married Archibald Ogilvy of Lawton and died on 25 December 1583.

The feud ended as it began. In 1552 Thomas was killed by James Ross, son and heir of the murdered James Ross of Craigton, with his kinsmen and friends, in revenge for his father’s death. Thomas’s brother John succeeded. The feud even made its way into Scots law: in Ld. Kinfauns v. Ld. Craigie (1552), recorded in Morison’s Dictionary of Decisions, the court held that a man whose goods had been plundered while he was an outlaw could sue for them once his outlawry was lifted.

3rd Lord Kinfauns
Sir John Charteris

John became lord because his brother was murdered, and he did not let it rest. He was served heir to Thomas on 10 April 1553, and on 11 March 1553 he and his brother’s widow, Katherine Graham, raised an action against the men responsible for the killing.

By 17 May 1551 he had married Janet, daughter of Sir James Chisholm, 2nd of Cromlix, and together they disponed their lands of Lumphanan to Mr Patrick Carnegy. Then came a turn nobody could have planned. His only surviving son, George, died before him, so he adopted Henry Lindsay as his heir, who appears afterwards as Henry Lindsay alias Charteris, filius adoptive of John Charteris of Kinfauns. John died about 1600.

George Charteris, younger of Kinfauns, the son who should have inherited, married Katherine, daughter of Alexander Blair, fiar of Balthayock, under a marriage contract of 11 July 1567. On 7 November 1575 he witnessed, with his father, a charter by Patrick Cheyne of Essilmont granting the lands of Essilmont to Helen Bruce, relict of George Hay, Earl of Errol, and Cheyne’s future wife. He died without children in his father’s lifetime, some time before 17 January 1578.

4th Lord Kinfauns
13th Earl of Crawford

An adopted heir, a wedding present and an earldom, all in one life. Henry Lindsay became Harry Charteris by adoption and held the lordship of Kinfauns until 1608, when he gave it, lands and all, to his son as a wedding present. The charter calls him Lord of Carelstoun. He later inherited the earldom of Crawford, though he enjoyed it only for the last two years of his life.

5th Lord Kinfauns
Sir John Lindsay

Not many people can say they were given a lordship as a wedding present. Sir John Lindsay was, and in 1608 the King confirmed it in a royal charter under the Great Seal. That King was James VI, who was by then also King James I of Great Britain, France and Ireland. Sadly Sir John died before his father.

Earls of Kinnoull

6th Lord Kinfauns
1st Earl of Kinnoull
Sir George Hay

With Sir George Hay, born in 1570, Kinfauns arrives at the very top of Scottish public life.

In 1620 he was infeft of the lordship of Kinfauns, becoming 6th Lord Kinfauns, a title tied to the barony. His charter gave him the keeping of the Tay, with a salmon from every boat. He was made Viscount of Dupplin in 1627 and Earl of Kinnoull in 1633 by King Charles I, and along the way he served as Lord Clerk Register, Privy Councillor and Lord Chancellor of Scotland. A Lord Kinfauns as Lord Chancellor of Scotland is hard to beat.

He married Margaret Halyburton and they had three children. His eldest son, Peter, died at Kinfauns in 1621, so it was his second son, George Hay, who inherited the titles and lands on his death in 1634.

7th Lord Kinfauns
2nd Earl of Kinnoull
Sir George Hay

Sir George Hay, born in 1596, was the only surviving son of the 1st Earl of Kinnoull and Margaret Halyburton. From 1621 to 1634 he was styled Lord Kinfauns as a courtesy title, taken from his father’s lordship, and in 1634 he succeeded as 2nd Earl of Kinnoull and 7th Lord Kinfauns.

He was a soldier first. He served as a captain in the Île de Ré expedition of 1628 and as a colonel at the siege of ’s-Hertogenbosch in 1629, and on 18 June 1629 the Dutch States-General swore him in as “Sir Gorge Hayes, baron van Kienfaunes”, his courtesy title turning up in a foreign army’s paperwork.

The detail fans of the title will like best comes in 1641, when Parliament ratified his infeftment in the Lordship of Kinfauns with its lands, hereditary rights and duties, among them the keeping of the waters of the Tay. That is the power of admiralty which the Lords of Kinfauns had over the Tay, written into an Act of Parliament.

At court he was Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard to King Charles I from 1632 to 1635 and a member of the King’s Privy Council, loyal to the Crown through a very turbulent period.

Sir George Hay died on 5 October 1644.

Blair and Carnegie Lordship

8th Lord Kinfauns
Sir Alexander Blair of Balthayock

With Sir Alexander the lordship passes to the Blairs. He succeeded to Kinfauns in 1647, and in February 1654 he and his wife, Dame Elizabeth Fotheringham, formally disponed the lands and lordship, with their rights and properties, to their kinsman William Blair, a younger son of the Blairs of Tarsappie, who was infeft of the lordship of Kinfauns. The grant spells out the hereditary rights that went with the title: the office of Baillie of the Tay, the teind sheaves and the salmon fishings on the river.

The Scottish Parliament ratified the transaction, which put it beyond challenge, and Sir Alexander backed it with his own lands of Balthayock, Nether Balmyles and Potento as security.

Knighted around 1650, he served as a Commissioner for the Sheriffdom of Perth in 1661 and as a Justice of the Peace in 1663, and in 1687 he was made a burgess of Arbroath. He died in 1692.

9th Lord Kinfauns
Sir William Blair of Kinfauns, 1st Baronet

Sir William Blair of Kinfauns was created 1st Baronet of Kinfauns in the Baronetage of Nova Scotia on 18 September 1666. The baronetcy was limited to the heirs male of his body, and there lay the problem: he had two daughters and no sons.

So the baronetcy would die with him, but Kinfauns did not have to. When his daughter Ann married the Hon. Alexander Carnegie in 1671, Sir William agreed in the marriage contract to resign the barony to the couple in return for £40,000, and a Crown charter followed in 1673.

10th Baroness of Kinfauns
Ann Blair

Here is the first woman on the list. Ann Blair, daughter of Sir William Blair of Kinfauns, married the Hon. Alexander Carnegie, son of David Carnegie, 2nd Earl of Northesk, on 7 August 1671. Under the marriage contract her father resigned the barony to the couple for £40,000, and they received a crown charter of the lands and barony of Kinfauns on 28 February 1673, which made Ann the 10th Baroness of Kinfauns. Ann died about 1687 and her husband in 1691. Their son, Alexander Blair Carnegie, succeeded as 11th Lord Kinfauns in 1695.

11th Lord Kinfauns
Alexander Blair Carnegie

Born about 1682, the son of Alexander Carnegie and Ann Blair, he inherited Kinfauns through his mother and joined the two family names as Blair Carnegie. He was served heir to his mother in 1695, and he was only fourteen or fifteen when he married two years later.

His bride, in 1697, was Jean Carnegie, daughter of James Carnegie of Finhaven, and their marriage produced one of the best court cases on this page. In Lady Kinfauns v. Lord Kinfauns (19 July 1711), her family had lent her 2,000 merks to take the warm baths in England for an illness her physicians feared would turn to palsy, and he refused to pay. The Lords of Session held that a husband must pay for his wife’s cure if his means allow. By December his estate was in the hands of his creditors, and she was awarded 1,150 merks. They had at least two daughters. One of them, Margaret, was born at Berwick in August 1720, after he had spent years abroad. The next heirs challenged her paternity in 1725 and lost, and in 1741 she married John, 11th Lord Gray, carrying Kinfauns into the Gray family.

Lords of Gray

12th Lord Kinfauns
John 11th Lord Gray

The Grays arrive by marriage. On 17 October 1741 John, 11th Lord Gray, married Margaret Blair, heiress of Kinfauns in Perthshire, and with her the lordship and property came into his family. They had five sons, two of whom died before their father and three of whom succeeded to the title in turn, and seven daughters. Their daughter Jean married Francis Stuart, 9th Earl of Moray, and that marriage is why the Earls of Moray inherited Kinfauns more than a century later.

13th Lord Kinfauns
Charles 12th Lord Gray

Charles, son of John, 11th Lord Gray, and Margaret Blair, succeeded his father in 1782. He had only four years as lord and died in 1786.

14th Lord Kinfauns
William 13th Lord Gray

William, Charles’s younger brother, took the title on his death in 1786 and held it for two decades, running the family estates until 1807.

15th Lord Kinfauns
14th Lord Gray
Sir Francis Gray

Francis, the youngest son of the 11th Lord Gray, assumed the title in 1807, the third brother in a row to do so. He sat in the House of Lords as a Scottish representative peer from 1812 to 1841 and served as Postmaster-General of Scotland. Kinfauns fans owe him most for the castle: the new Kinfauns Castle, built in the 1820s, was his project.

16th Lord Kinfauns
John 15th Lord Gray

John, son of Francis, followed his father into the House of Lords as a Scottish representative peer from 1847 to 1867, and was a Deputy Lieutenant of Perthshire. He carried on developing the Kinfauns estate.

17th Baroness of Kinfauns
Madelina 16th Lady Gray

Madelina, John’s sister, inherited on his death. She never married, and held the title as Lady Gray until she died in 1869.

18th Baroness of Kinfauns
17th Lady Gray
Margaret Murray

Margaret was Madelina’s niece, the daughter of John Grant of Kilgraston and the Hon. Margaret Gray, and the widow of Major the Hon. David Henry Murray, a son of the 3rd Earl of Mansfield. Her memorial is one of the best pieces of evidence on this whole page: it records that she was “the 18th in succession to the barony which now merges into the Earldom of Moray”. It happened in two steps. When she died in 1878, the Lordship of Gray went to her cousin, the 14th Earl of Moray, and Kinfauns passed by entail to Edmund Stuart, who took the name Gray. When Edmund became 15th Earl in 1895, Kinfauns and the earldom came together for good, and the Lordship of Gray went off down another line.

Earls of Moray

19th Lord Kinfauns
15th Earl of Moray
Edmund Archibald Stuart Gray

From here the lords of Kinfauns are Earls of Moray. Edmund, a cousin descended from the 11th Lord Gray, married Anna Mary Collinson on 6 September 1877. He succeeded to the Lordship of Kinfauns in 1878 and to the Earldom of Moray in 1895. He died on 11 June 1901 with no direct heirs, and his younger brother, Francis James Stuart, succeeded him.

20th Lord Kinfauns
16th Earl of Moray
Lieut. Col Francis James Stuart

Lieutenant Colonel Francis James Stuart was a soldier before he was an earl, reaching that rank in the Liverpool Regiment.

On 24 June 1879 he married Gertrude Floyer Smith, daughter of the Reverend Francis Smith and Mary Isabella Bogue. They had no children.

He inherited in 1901 on the death of his elder brother, Edmund Archibald Stuart, 15th Earl of Moray. As earl he was a Justice of the Peace for Dorset, Fife and Perthshire and a Deputy Lieutenant of Perthshire.

He died on 20 November 1909, aged 66, and was succeeded by his younger brother, Morton Gray Stuart, as 17th Earl of Moray.

21st Lord Kinfauns
17th Earl of Moray
Morton Gray Stuart

Born on 16 April 1855 in Blandford, Dorset, Morton was the youngest son of the Reverend Edmund Luttrell Stuart and Elizabeth Jackson, and took a Master of Arts degree at Cambridge.

In 1890 he married Edith Douglas Palmer, daughter of Rear-Admiral George Palmer. They had four children, two of whom would become Earls of Moray in their turn. He succeeded his brother, Francis James Stuart, as 17th Earl in 1909 and took on the family estates, including Darnaway Castle and Kinfauns Castle.

He was the gardener of the family. A passionate horticulturist, he greatly improved the gardens at Kinfauns, where the walled garden was laid out in 1910. He died at Darnaway Castle on 19 April 1930 and was succeeded by his eldest son, Francis Douglas Stuart, as 18th Earl. After his death the Kinfauns estate was sold off and the castle passed out of the family, though the title stayed with the Earls of Moray.

22nd Lord Kinfauns
18th Earl of Moray
Francis Douglas Stuart

Francis Douglas Stuart was born on 10 July 1892, the eldest son of Morton Gray Stuart, 17th Earl of Moray, and Edith Douglas Palmer. In the First World War he served as a Captain in the Scottish Horse and in the Royal Air Force, and won the Military Cross.

On 21 June 1924 he married Barbara Murray of New York. They had three daughters: Lady Mary Anne Stuart, Lady Sarah Gray Stuart and Lady Arabella Stuart.

He succeeded his father as 18th Earl in 1930 and was appointed Lord-Lieutenant of Morayshire on 2 August 1935. He held the post until his death on 9 July 1943, the day before his 51st birthday.

23rd Lord Kinfauns
19th Earl of Moray
Archibald John Morton Stuart

Born in Edinburgh on 14 November 1894, he was the second son of the 17th Earl of Moray, Morton Gray Stuart, and Edith Douglas Palmer. He was a navy man: educated at the Royal Naval Colleges, he served in the Royal Navy in both World Wars and reached the rank of Lieutenant-Commander.

In 1922 he married Mabel Helen Maud Wilson, and they had four children, among them his successor, Douglas John Moray Stuart. He inherited the earldom in 1943 on the death of his elder brother, Francis.

He served as Deputy Lieutenant of Morayshire and died in Forres on 27 March 1974, aged 79.

24th Lord Kinfauns
20th Earl of Moray
Douglas John Moray Stuart

The 24th lord had the most unexpected childhood of any of them. Born in Johannesburg on 13 February 1928, he spent his early childhood on the family’s cattle ranch in Bechuanaland, now Botswana, went to school in South Africa, and only came to Scotland in 1945. He studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and the Royal Agricultural College, and went on to lead the Moray Estates Development Company through years of diversification and modernisation.

In 1964 he married Lady Malvina Murray, and they had two children, one of them John Douglas Stuart, the 21st Earl. He was also a lifelong car enthusiast, and founded both the Doune Motor Museum and the Doune Hill Climb.

In 1984 he placed Doune Castle in the care of Historic Scotland. He died on 23 September 2011.

25th Lord Kinfauns
21st Earl of Moray
John Douglas Stuart

John Douglas Stuart is the son of Douglas John Moray Stuart, 20th Earl of Moray, and Lady Malvina Murray, and was educated at Loretto School and University College London.

As 21st Earl he has overseen the Moray Estates Development Company, carrying on the work of modernising and diversifying the family’s landholdings, and he looks after historic properties such as Darnaway Castle.

Like some early Scottish titles, Kinfauns can pass to a successor named by its holder. The Earl of Moray conferred and bestowed his subsidiary title Lord Kinfauns by nomination.

Commane Lordship

26th Lord Kinfauns Chief of Ó Comáin

The 26th lord brings the title to County Clare. Fergus Commane is Chief of Clan Ó Comáin, an Irish clan recognised in 2025 by Clans of Ireland, whose patron at the time was Michael D. Higgins, President of Ireland. Its line is traced to the early kings of the Déisi of Munster and to the medieval chiefdom of Tulach Commáin. He married Maria Nadolski, with whom he had three children, and her family, Warzęta Korwin z Ślepowrony, has been counted among the Polish nobility since 1224. So the present lords of Kinfauns have an Irish chief on one side and a Polish noble house on the other.

A Clare man and a Roman Catholic, he grew up as one of eleven children on his parents’ farm, on land that had once belonged to Newhall, the big house across the fields, and about fifteen miles from Cahercommaun, the great stone fort that was the old capital of the chiefdom. Like many young Irish people of his generation he left for London, and he did well in business there. By all accounts he never stopped wanting Newhall, and years later the house was his.

Since then Newhall has been a family home again. With his wife, Maria, Lady Kinfauns, he has taken on the care of the Holy Well of St John and of Killone Abbey, both on the estate, and in November 2025 planning permission was granted to turn the old stable block into a wellness retreat built around the folklore and history of the place. Anyone who knows how many Irish country houses were lost in the last century will see why this one counts as a rare happy ending.

Then there is the title. In 2024 Fergus succeeded as 26th Lord Kinfauns by nomination, which is a way for some early Scottish titles of this kind to pass. Before that the lordship had sat for generations among the titles of the Earls of Moray, one of Scotland’s seven ancient earldoms. He does not use the title, and goes by his chiefly title instead. Read more about the title and Newhall here.

The clan side is just as striking. The recognition of Clan Commane Ó Comáin by Clans of Ireland rested on DNA matching and on academic research into the Chiefdom of Tulach Commáin in County Clare, and Fergus had a hand in bringing it about. He was then consecrated as Taoiseach, chosen by the derbhfine in the old Brehon-law manner, the traditional way a Gaelic clan picks its chief. The clan held its first gathering in June 2026 at Killone, with Mass said at the holy well.
